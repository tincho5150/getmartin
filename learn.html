Buyer Psychology How people actually make decisions

B2B buyers are not rational actors who evaluate options objectively and choose the best one. They are people — under pressure, managing risk, protecting their careers, navigating internal politics. Understanding the mental models that drive real buying decisions is what separates marketers who create genuine urgency from those who just add more slides to the deck.

75% of B2B buyers complete the majority of their research before speaking to a rep. By the time they reach out, they've already formed a view. Your job is to shape that view — not change it after the fact.

Implication The sale happens in the research phase. If you're not present there — through content, tools, reputation — you're not in the conversation.
02 Status Quo Bias Decision-making +

Perceived gain from switching

+1×

Perceived pain of switching

−2.5×

The pain of change feels 2–3× heavier than the equivalent gain

Doing nothing is always the default. It requires no decision, no risk, no justification. Your product has to make the cost of inaction feel more painful than the cost of change — or the deal dies quietly with "we'll revisit next quarter."

Implication Don't just show the benefit of switching. Make the hidden cost of staying visible — quantified, felt, and personal to their operation.
03 Loss Aversion Positioning +

Gain framing

"Our platform could increase your team's output by 30%"

Sounds good. We'll think about it.

Loss framing

"Your team is losing the equivalent of one full day per person, per week, to this problem."

Tell me more. What would that cost us annually?

People feel losses roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. The same information framed as what they're losing — rather than what they could gain — produces a fundamentally different emotional response and a faster decision.

Implication Reframe your value proposition around what the buyer is currently losing — time, money, deals, talent — not what they could gain.

Your champion walks into a room of unconvinced stakeholders — without you

Your champion believes in you. But they have to sell internally to people you've never met, with objections you've never heard, using arguments you've never equipped them with. Most champions lose that room — not because the product is wrong, but because they weren't ready to win it.

Implication Give your champion something that sells without you in the room — a document, a tool, a one-pager built for their committee, not for you.
05 Social Proof in B2B Trust +

Low trust

Buyers don't trust what companies say about themselves — they trust what people like them say. A specific result from a recognisable company does more commercial work than any claim you can make about your own product.

Implication Collect specific, numbered results from real customers. "We helped them improve efficiency" is worthless. "23% reduction in spoilage in Q1" is a sales asset.
Sales Process How the sales motion works — the stages, the traps, the mechanics

Most B2B sales processes fail at predictable moments — the discovery call that turns into a pitch, the demo shown too early, the internal champion who can't close the committee. This track maps the mechanics of how complex B2B deals move — or stall — and where the decisions that determine the outcome are actually made.

01

Discovery

The most important conversation in the sales process — and the one most often turned into a pitch. Discovery is about understanding the buyer's world: what the problem costs them, why they haven't solved it, and whether you're actually the right fit.

Goal: understand, not pitch Questions over features Why now matters
The trap: turning discovery into a demo. The moment you start showing the product, you've stopped listening — and the buyer notices.
02

Qualification

Not every prospect is a real opportunity. Qualification is the discipline of establishing — early — whether the budget, authority, need, and timing are real. Chasing unqualified deals wastes both sides' time and kills your close rate.

Budget confirmed Decision-maker identified Timeline is vague = risk
The trap: hoping qualification will sort itself out later. It won't. Deals that aren't qualified early become deals that disappear at proposal stage.
03

The Demo

Shown too early, the demo kills deals. Shown to the wrong person, it produces no decision. The demo should be a confirmation of what the buyer already suspects is true — not the moment you try to convince them. By the time they see the product, they should already want it.

Too early = fatal Tailored to their problem Right stakeholders in room
The trap: a generic feature walkthrough. If the demo could be for anyone, it lands for no one. Show them their problem being solved — not your product's capabilities.
04

The Proposal

A proposal is not a price list. It's a business case — written for a committee, not the person you've been speaking to. It should restate the problem in their language, quantify the cost of inaction, and make the ROI obvious to someone who has never met you.

Written for the committee Cost of inaction visible Price without context = stall
The trap: sending a proposal without a walkthrough. A PDF sent cold gets read cold. Walk your champion through it so they can walk the room through it.
05

The Close

If the previous four stages were done well, the close is a confirmation — not a negotiation. The deal is won or lost in Discovery, not here. The best closes happen when the buyer feels that saying yes is the obvious, low-risk, well-understood next step.

Confirmation, not persuasion Objections already handled Urgency manufactured = churn
The trap: artificial urgency. Discounts and deadlines that appear from nowhere signal that the value was never real. Close on confidence, not pressure.
