TOFU, MOFU, BOFU

Top of funnel, middle of funnel, bottom of funnel. These three stages describe where a buyer is in their journey — and what kind of content or conversation they need at each stage. TOFU is awareness — broad, educational. MOFU is evaluation — comparative, specific. BOFU is decision — proof, ROI, risk reduction. Most companies only create content for one stage and wonder why the others stall.

Brand vs. Performance

Performance marketing drives measurable, immediate results — clicks, leads, conversions. Brand marketing builds the long-term reputation that makes performance marketing cheaper and more effective over time. Most early-stage companies default to performance only because it's measurable. B2B buyers are not rational actors. They are people — under pressure, managing risk, protecting their careers. Understanding the mental models that drive real buying decisions is what separates genuine urgency from adding more slides to the deck.

The Buying Journey

75% of the decision happens before they talk to you

B2B buyers complete most of their research independently. By the time they reach out, they've already formed a view and often have a preferred vendor. Your job is to shape that view during the research phase — not overcome it after the fact.

of the buying journey is self-directed, without vendor contact Gartner, 2024

Status Quo Bias

Doing nothing always wins — unless you make the cost visible

The default decision in any buying process is inaction. Change requires effort, risk, and internal justification. Your product has to make the cost of staying put feel more painful than the cost of moving — or the deal dies quietly with "we'll revisit next quarter."

Loss Aversion

Losses feel twice as powerful as equivalent gains

Reframe your pitch around what the buyer is currently losing — not what they could gain. Same information, completely different urgency.

The reframe

"Your team is losing one full day per person, per week."

vs. "Our platform could increase output by 30%." Same fact. Completely different emotional response.

Data

2×

The Champion Problem

Your champion has to sell your deal without you in the room

Your champion believes in you. But they face a committee of people you've never met, with objections you've never heard, using arguments you've never given them. Most champions lose that room — not because the product is wrong, but because they weren't equipped to win it.

Social Proof

What others say does more work than anything you say

Buyers don't trust what companies say about themselves. A specific result from a recognisable company does more commercial work than any claim you can make. "23% reduction in spoilage in Q1" is a sales asset. "We helped them improve efficiency" is noise.

The difference

"23% reduction in spoilage in Q1. I wish we'd done this two years ago."

Specific. Numbered. From a real person at a real company. That's a sales asset. Anything less is wallpaper.

Most B2B sales processes fail at predictable moments. This track maps how complex deals move — or stall — and where the decisions that determine the outcome are actually made.

01 — Discovery

The most important call you'll ever turn into a pitch

Discovery is about understanding the buyer's world — what the problem costs, why they haven't solved it, whether you're the right fit. The moment you start showing the product, you've stopped listening.

The rule

Questions over features. Always.

You should be speaking less than 30% of the time. If you're pitching, you've already lost the discovery.

02 — Qualification

Not every prospect is a real opportunity

Establish early whether budget, authority, need, and timing are real. Deals that aren't qualified early disappear at proposal stage — after weeks of work. The best sales teams disqualify faster than they qualify.

03 — The Demo

By the time they see it, they should already want it

Shown too early, the demo kills deals. It should confirm what the buyer already suspects — not convince someone who isn't ready. A generic feature walkthrough lands for no one.

The test

Could this demo be for anyone?

If yes, it lands for no one. Show them their problem being solved — not your product's capabilities.

04 — The Proposal

A business case for people you've never met

A proposal is not a price list. It's a document written for the committee — people who weren't in the room. It should restate the problem in their language, quantify the cost of inaction, and make the ROI obvious to someone who has never spoken to you. Walk your champion through it before you send it cold.

05 — The Close

The deal is won or lost in Discovery — not here

If the previous four stages were done well, the close is a confirmation, not a negotiation. The best closes happen when saying yes feels like the obvious, low-risk, well-understood next step.

The trap

Artificial urgency signals the value was never real

Discounts and deadlines that appear from nowhere destroy trust. Close on confidence, not pressure.
